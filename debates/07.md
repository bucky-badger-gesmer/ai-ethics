## Scenario

Teams are shipping models that hire, lend, triage patients, score defendants, and grade students. Building those systems more fairly can mean more data work, more testing, slower release, and, in some cases, a drop in a chosen accuracy metric. The prompt asks you to pick a priority: fairness, even if advancement slows, or innovation, with some ethical risk accepted as the price of progress.

## Preparation

Identify areas in practice that may be most affected by a tradeoff between fairness and innovation. Consider differing measures of fairness (including benefits and limitations), model accuracy, human impact, challenges in practice, and past discussions of harm and bias. Finally, examine the tradeoff claim itself: does it have merit? What can be done to improve outcomes?

No side was assigned, and Canvas lists no rubric or required readings.

**Framing reminders:**

- The question assumes a tradeoff. Week 9's first question is "Does optimizing for fairness by definition reduce accuracy?" and the answer is "Sometimes!" Interrogate the claim before you pick a side.
- "Fairness" is not one number. Week 9 says some measures "are incompatible with other measures of fairness." A lot of the heat in this debate is fairness versus fairness, not fairness versus progress.
- "Ethical risk" and "innovation" are doing a lot of work. Risk to whom? Innovation that gets scrapped (Amazon) or that scores the wrong target (Obermeyer) is not durable progress.
- Week 8's line still applies: "Are we using this model to learn about the training data, or to create/predict something that will impact a person's life or livelihood?" High-stakes impact is where "accept ethical risk" is hardest to defend.
- Week 1 and Week 8 both say we are "not building AI for AI's sake." Speed is not the course's standard of success; human impact is.

## Fairness measures (benefits and limits)

Week 9 defines two core measures and then warns that they can conflict. Use this table to keep the definitions straight.

| Measure | What it asks | Benefit | Limit |
| ------- | ------------ | ------- | ----- |
| **Statistical parity** (Week 9, slide 5) | "No significant difference in predictions by class/group." Example: "same percentage of men and women receive job offers." | Easy to explain; targets unequal access to an opportunity (hiring, lending, callbacks). | Ignores whether groups have different base rates or qualifications. Can look "fair" while error rates are lopsided. |
| **Equalized odds / "Equal Odds"** (Week 9, slide 5; Hardt, Price & Srebro, 2016) | "No significant difference in prediction by class/group given an outcome." True-positive and true-negative rates (and therefore false-positive and false-negative rates) are the same across groups. | Ties fairness to actual outcomes: qualified people are accepted at the same rate, and errors are shared. | Needs a trustworthy outcome label. Incompatible with calibration/predictive parity when base rates differ (Kleinberg 2016; Chouldechova 2017). |
| **Calibration / predictive parity** | A score of 7 means about the same chance of the outcome in every group. This is what Northpointe emphasized for COMPAS. | Matches how decision-makers use risk scores; "equally accurate" in a predictive sense. | When prevalence differs, this produces unequal false-positive and false-negative rates (Chouldechova 2017). That is ProPublica's finding. |
| **Adverse impact / 80% (four-fifths) rule** (Week 9, slide 6) | A group's selection rate should be at least 80% of the most-selected group's rate. From the EEOC [Uniform Guidelines](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XIV/part-1607/subject-group-ECFRdb347e844acdea6/section-1607.4). | Legal and operational hook in employment; looks at real decisions, not only model scores. | A rule of thumb, not a complete definition of fairness. Can miss harm inside a group that still clears 80%, and can flag noise in small samples. |
| **Accuracy by class, plus qualitative review** (Week 8 and Week 9, slide 6) | "Evaluate accuracy by class/group" and "unique aspects of each class/group." Week 8: "Use multiple metrics, not just model accuracy." | Catches failures a single headline number hides. Week 8's resume study needed both quantitative and qualitative methods. | More work; still doesn't tell you which metric should govern a release. |

Week 9, slide 8: "Some measures of fairness are incompatible with other measures of fairness (statistical parity vs. distribution of errors)." Slide 15: "Fairness is not universally defined. And some measures of fairness may conflict with other measures!" That is the course's own impossibility result, stated in plain language.

The slides do not themselves prove the Kleinberg or Chouldechova theorems, or discuss Rodolfa et al.; those are outside research, and Week 9's references list does point to Chouldechova ("Fair prediction with disparate impact"), Hardt et al. ("Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning"), and a paper titled "Is There a Tradeoff Between Fairness and Accuracy?" (Dutta et al., 2020).

## Areas of practice most affected

The tradeoff, when it appears, is sharpest where a model changes someone's life and historical data is already skewed.

| Domain | Why the tradeoff shows up | What "moving fast" has already cost |
| ------ | ------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **Hiring and promotion** | Past hiring is unbalanced (Week 2, slide 11 lists "Job qualifications"). A model trained to copy that history will look accurate and still screen out qualified people in underrepresented groups. | Amazon's résumé tool, trained on a decade of mostly male applications, "taught itself that male candidates were preferable" and penalized résumés that included the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club captain"). It was scrapped. |
| **Lending and credit** | Week 2 also flags "Mortgage/loan approvals." Approval rates and default labels reflect who was allowed to borrow, not only who would repay. | Statistical parity can clash with default prediction. Errors here mean lost homes or denied credit. The EU AI Act treats credit-scoring AI as high-risk. |
| **Healthcare** | A convenient label (cost, utilization) can be highly "accurate" and still the wrong target. | Obermeyer et al. (2019): a widely used algorithm was accurate at predicting cost and still systematically under-referred Black patients. Remedying the disparity would raise the share of Black patients among those flagged for extra help from 17.7% to 46.5%. |
| **Criminal justice** | Recidivism base rates differ across groups, so popular fairness metrics cannot all hold (Chouldechova 2017). False positives mean longer detention; false negatives mean missed risk. | COMPAS: ProPublica found Black defendants who did not reoffend were labeled higher risk at 44.9%, versus 23.5% of white defendants. Northpointe replied that the scores were calibrated. Both can be true. |
| **Education** | Week 9's Turnitin case: even a low error rate is heavy when "someone's grade and reputation are at stake." | The fairness question is not only the model's error rate. It is "the way in which we use this model." |
| **Generative AI and consumer products** | Week 8 and Week 9 ask why biased results are so easy to find, and why models ship "with clear and present issues." Lower stakes per person, but huge scale. | Stereotypes in images, résumés, and recommendations. Week 8: "Bias is a very practical problem and has major human impact!" |

The EU AI Act's high-risk list tracks this table almost exactly: employment and worker management, creditworthiness, education, law enforcement, and essential public services ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), recitals 56–59 and Annex III).

## Research evidence

| Claim | What the evidence says |
| ----- | ---------------------- |
| **Fairness metrics can be mutually impossible** | Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016): three natural criteria (calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, balance for the negative class) cannot hold at once except in special cases: perfect prediction, or equal base rates across groups. Chouldechova (2017): predictive parity and error-rate balance cannot all be satisfied when recidivism prevalence differs by group. |
| **COMPAS / ProPublica** | ProPublica (Angwin et al., 2016): among people labeled higher risk who did not reoffend, the rate was 44.9% for Black defendants and 23.5% for white defendants; among those labeled lower risk who did reoffend, 28.0% vs. 47.7%. Overall, 61% of those deemed likely to reoffend were arrested within two years. Northpointe/Equivant (Dieterich, Mendoza & Brennan, 2016) replied that the tool was calibrated and comparably accurate by race. Chouldechova shows both findings can be mathematically consistent. |
| **The fairness–accuracy tradeoff is often overstated** | Rodolfa, Lamba & Ghani (2021, *Nature Machine Intelligence*), in four resource-constrained policy settings (mental health outreach, housing inspections, student outcomes, education crowdfunding): "fairness–accuracy trade-offs to be negligible in practice." After post-hoc adjustments to equalize recall, "fairness was substantially improved without sacrificing accuracy." |
| **A tradeoff can be an artifact of biased labels** | Dutta et al. (2020), the "Is There a Trade-Off Between Fairness and Accuracy?" paper flagged on Week 9's references slide: there always exist ideal (unbiased) distributions such that optimal fairness and accuracy hold together. A tradeoff appears when accuracy is scored on biased observed data. They conclude it is "problematic to measure accuracy with respect to data that reflects bias." |
| **Wrong proxy, "accurate" model, unfair impact** | Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli & Mullainathan (2019, *Science*): a commercial health algorithm "exhibits significant racial bias." At a given risk score, Black patients were sicker than white patients. "The bias arises because the algorithm predicts health care costs rather than illness." Remedying it "would increase the percentage of Black patients receiving additional help from 17.7 to 46.5%." Cost looked like a good accuracy target and still produced the disparity. |
| **Innovation on biased history can fail outright** | Reuters / BBC (2018): Amazon's experimental hiring engine, trained on ten years of applications that mostly came from men, "taught itself that male candidates were preferable" and penalized résumés containing the word "women's." Amazon edited the model to be neutral to those terms, could not guarantee it would not find other ways to discriminate, and abandoned the project. |
| **Algorithms can beat biased human judgment** | Kleinberg, Lakkaraju, Leskovec, Ludwig & Mullainathan (2018, *QJE*; NBER WP 23180): on New York City bail decisions, a policy simulation shows jail populations could fall about 42% with no increase in crime, or crime fall about 25% with no change in jailing rates, "while also significantly reducing the percentage of African-Americans and Hispanics in jail." Side A's strongest evidence that delay has a human cost. Side B's reply: the gains came from careful evaluation against outcomes and by group, which is fairness work. |
| **Hardt et al. on equal opportunity** | Hardt, Price & Srebro (2016) formalize equality of opportunity / equalized odds and give a post-processing method to enforce it on an existing predictor while minimizing loss. Fairness constraints are not only a brake; they are an adjustment you can apply after you have a model. |
| **Regulation tries to hold both goals** | The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689, recital 1) aims "to promote the uptake of human centric and trustworthy artificial intelligence (AI) while ensuring a high level of protection of health, safety, fundamental rights ... and to support innovation." Recital 25 excludes AI developed solely for scientific research from the Act's scope, and Article 57 requires regulatory sandboxes. High-risk rules are reserved for the domains above, not every model. |
| **NIST does not treat accuracy as a trump card** | NIST AI RMF 1.0: trustworthy AI should be "valid and reliable" and also "fair with harmful bias managed." "Creating trustworthy AI requires balancing each of these characteristics based on the AI system’s context of use." "Highly secure but unfair systems, accurate but opaque and uninterpretable systems, and inaccurate but secure, privacy-enhanced, and transparent systems are all undesirable." Fairness "can be complex and difficult to define because perceptions of fairness differ among cultures," which matches Week 9's "no universal consensus." |

## Does the tradeoff claim have merit?

**Yes, in part, but the prompt overstates it.**

1. **Fairness versus fairness is the sharper tradeoff.** Kleinberg and Chouldechova are not "fairness versus accuracy" results. They show that popular group-fairness definitions collide when base rates differ and prediction is imperfect. COMPAS is the worked example: calibrated and unequally error-prone at the same time. Prioritizing "fairness" still requires saying which fairness, and to whom the errors fall.

2. **Fairness versus accuracy is real sometimes, and not a law of nature.** Week 9 says "Sometimes!" twice, then "not always" and "optimize for both where possible." Rodolfa et al. found the accuracy cost of equalizing recall to be negligible in four real allocation problems. Dutta et al. show the apparent cost can be an artifact of scoring accuracy on biased data. Obermeyer shows a model can be accurate at the wrong thing.

3. **Innovation versus fairness is often the weakest version of the claim.** Amazon didn't face a tragic choice between a slightly less accurate hiring model and a fairer one. It built a tool that learned yesterday's inequality, could not be trusted, and was thrown out. That delayed hiring automation more than a fairness threshold would have. Unfair systems also invite regulation, lawsuits, and lost trust, which are not a shortcut to progress.

4. **Delay has costs too, and they belong in the argument.** There are settings (medical triage tools with validated labels, disaster response, accessibility) where waiting has a human price, and where a more accurate model can reduce harm overall. Side A is on firm ground when it refuses to treat every release gate as costless. It is on weak ground when it treats "ethical risk" as a general toll that affected people should pay so the field can move faster.

**Net:** the claim has merit as "you may not be able to satisfy every metric at once, and in some settings a fairness constraint will cost some accuracy on the observed label." It does not have merit as "we must accept harm to people in order to innovate."

## What can be done to improve outcomes

These are the moves both sides can agree on, and they are mostly already in the slides.

- **Set release requirements, including a fairness threshold.** Week 9, slide 14: "Define your requirements for model release. Threshold for fairness?"
- **Ask who the model works for, and which errors it makes.** Same slide: "Who does the model work for? Who benefits? Who does it fail for? What type of errors are you seeing?"
- **Fix the label and the data, not only the post-hoc constraint.** Obermeyer: switching from cost to a health-need label removed most of the bias. Week 2: represent the population the model will affect; imbalance in historical hiring, lending, and enforcement is a known trap.
- **Use multiple metrics, and measure adverse impact on actual decisions.** Week 8: "Use multiple metrics, not just model accuracy." Week 9: assess adverse impact (80% rule) and accuracy by group.
- **Use post-processing when it is cheap.** Hardt et al. and Rodolfa et al. show group-specific thresholds can often equalize error or recall without building a brand-new model and, in Rodolfa's settings, without a meaningful accuracy drop.
- **Don't confuse "ignore demographics" with fairness.** Week 9, slide 7: excluding sensitive attributes does not automatically ensure fairness, and it can conflict with privacy and with auditing.
- **Review in a multi-disciplinary team, in context.** Week 4: "AI risks should be examined by a multi-disciplinary team based on context, not done in isolation." NIST: human judgment should set the metrics and the thresholds.
- **Regulate by risk, not by slogan.** The EU AI Act puts heavy duties on high-risk uses (hiring, credit, education, law enforcement) and still "supports innovation" through research exclusions and sandboxes. That is a structured answer to "must we slow everything?"
- **Treat "looks good" versus "is good" as a release test.** Week 9's burger-ad slide is not a joke: marketing claims are not performance.

## Positions

### Innovation should be the priority

I believe ethical AI development should prioritize innovation, and should accept that some ethical risk comes with progress. Week 9 is honest that optimizing for fairness "sometimes" reduces accuracy, and that the tradeoff can remain even after you debias the training data. In those cases, a less accurate model is also a less useful one: it misses patients, misallocates inspections, or blocks tools that would have been, on net, better than the status quo. Kleinberg et al. (2018) found that a machine-learning bail model, compared with judges' actual decisions in New York City, could reduce the jail population by about 42% with no increase in crime, while also reducing the share of Black and Hispanic defendants in jail. Holding that tool back would leave people in jail under a human process that is less accurate and less fair.

Fairness is also not a single target. Kleinberg et al. and Chouldechova show that calibration and equal error rates cannot generally be satisfied together. If we halt or slow development until every fairness metric is green, we will never ship, because the math doesn't allow it unless we have perfect prediction or equal base rates. Northpointe's reply to ProPublica is a reminder that a tool can be "unfair" on one definition and well calibrated on another.

Delay is not ethically free. The EU AI Act itself tries to protect rights *and* "support innovation," including by leaving research and sandboxes room to operate. The constructive version of this side is not "ignore harm." It is: ship with monitoring, improve with better data (Cowgill; Week 2), and don't let an unresolved philosophical dispute about metrics become a veto on useful systems.

### Fairness should be the priority

I believe ethical AI development should prioritize fairness, even when that slows a release. Week 1 and Week 8 say this course is about human impact, not building AI for its own sake. Week 9 says data scientists have "the ethical necessity of considering societal fairness" even though there is no universal definition, and it asks whether a model "just look[s] good or is it actually good."

The tradeoff in the prompt is weaker than it sounds. Week 9 says it is "not always" real and tells us to "optimize for both where possible." Rodolfa et al. found the accuracy cost of improving fairness "negligible in practice" in four policy settings. When a painful tradeoff does show up, it is often because we are measuring accuracy on a biased label, which is Dutta et al.'s point and Obermeyer's case: a cost model can look accurate and still flag Black patients for extra care at well under half the rate a fair model would (17.7% vs. 46.5% of those flagged). Amazon's hiring tool shows what "innovation first" looks like on biased history: the product had to be abandoned.

Kleinberg and Chouldechova matter, but they cut the other way too. If fairness metrics conflict, the adult response is to pick the measure that matches the harm (false positives in pretrial detention; equal opportunity in hiring) and set a release threshold, which is exactly what Week 9 recommends. The EU AI Act and the NIST AI RMF both treat fairness and harmful bias as core, not optional add-ons after the product ships.

### Debate portion

**Opening (about 1 minute):** Thesis, the three points in one sentence each, then one concrete case (Obermeyer or Amazon for fairness; COMPAS metric conflict or delayed beneficial tools for innovation).

**The three places the argument actually turns:**

1. **Is the tradeoff real?** Side A: Week 9 says sometimes, and those are the cases that matter. Side B: often small, often an artifact of the label, and Week 9 says optimize for both.
2. **Fairness of what?** Side A: you can't prioritize a concept that disagrees with itself (Kleinberg / Chouldechova / COMPAS). Side B: then choose the metric that matches the harm, and still don't ship with known adverse impact.
3. **Who pays for speed?** Side A: the public also pays if useful systems are late. Side B: "ethical risk" is not abstract; it is a person who is denied care, a job, credit, or liberty.

**Questions to ask the innovation side:**

- "Week 9 asks, if you really can't have both, then what? Who is allowed to be the group the model fails for?"
- "Amazon's tool was innovative and still had to be scrapped. How is that faster progress than a fairness gate?"
- "Obermeyer's algorithm was accurate at predicting cost. Is that the accuracy you'd be willing to protect at the expense of fairness?"
