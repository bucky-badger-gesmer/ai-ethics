# Debate 04 — Who Should Control Personal Data?

**Course:** SEIS 651: AI Ethics · University of St. Thomas

|                |                                                                                                                                       |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Question**   | Which is better: company freedom after collecting personal data, or individual knowledge and control over its existence and handling? |
| **Position 1** | Companies have the right to do whatever they wish with personal data after collecting it.                                             |
| **Position 2** | Individuals have the right to know about personal data concerning them and control whether it exists and how it is handled.           |

## Quick card

Read this right before you speak.

|                    | Side A: Company control                                                                                              | Side B: Individual control                                                     |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Best thesis**    | Clear voluntary agreements should delegate downstream decisions to the company that builds and operates the service. | People should retain enforceable rights over information about them.           |
| **Point 1**        | Company investment helps create the value.                                                                           | Sharing for one purpose isn't surrendering every future choice.                |
| **Point 2**        | Future benefits can't always be predicted at collection; individual vetoes can block collective gains.               | The company earns revenue; the person bears exposure.                          |
| **Point 3**        | Meaningful agreements authorize downstream decisions without constant reapproval.                                    | Inferences and models can expose people beyond the original record.            |
| **Main challenge** | Explain why general accountability protects people better than individual vetoes.                                    | Explain how strong control accommodates evidence and public-interest uses.     |
| **Closing line**   | “Honor the agreement, let the company create value, and hold it responsible for harm.”                               | “Information about me should not become power over me without accountability.” |

## Preparation

Use the **same concrete cases** for both sides. Ask who benefits, who bears the risk, what people were told, and whether a less intrusive use achieves the same goal.

Keep **ethical judgment separate from current law**. Legal rules show workable boundaries; they don't automatically prove which approach is morally better.

**Framing warning:** Both positions are stated in absolute terms, and neither holds up literally. Side A's defensible version is delegated authority under clear, voluntarily accepted terms plus liability for harm—not literally anything goes, and not an unlimited legal right. Side B's defensible version is strong individual rights with justified exceptions—not an absolute veto. The real dispute is who decides first and how misuse gets challenged.

## Framing corrections (if asked)

- **Germany isn't socialist in this comparison.** It has a **social market economy**: markets and competition combined with regulation and social protection. That label doesn't by itself explain its privacy rules. ([German public explainer](https://www.deutschland.de/en/topic/business/social-market-economy-in-germany-growth-and-prosperity))
- **For a 2015 setting, don't backdate GDPR.** Directive 95/46/EC already required purpose limits, information, access, and qualified correction/objection rights through national implementation. GDPR was adopted in 2016 and applied from May 25, 2018. ([Directive, Arts. 6–14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:31995L0046); [GDPR, Arts. 94–99](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))
- **The US wasn't a legal free-for-all in 2015.** FTC unfairness/deception enforcement and sector-specific rules already constrained companies. The useful contrast is broader European protection versus a more sectoral US approach, not rights versus no rights. ([FTC 2015 review](https://www.ftc.gov/reports/privacy-data-security-update-2015))
- **Privacy isn't the same as absolute property ownership.** GDPR establishes individual rights and controller duties, not unlimited dominion over every fact mentioning someone. A company's investment likewise doesn't establish ownership of a person's identity. ([GDPR, recital 4 and Arts. 12–21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))
- **Consent isn't the only lawful basis.** GDPR also permits necessary contractual processing, legal obligations, vital interests, public-interest tasks, and qualified legitimate interests. Putting something in the terms doesn't automatically make it contractually necessary; sensitive categories face additional restrictions. ([GDPR, Arts. 6 and 9](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))
- **Protected rights aren't an absolute veto.** Erasure has exceptions for necessary legal retention, legal claims, expression, specified public-health purposes, and protected research. Strictly necessary fraud prevention can qualify as a legitimate interest, subject to balancing—not unlimited retention. ([GDPR, Art. 17 and recital 47](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))
- **US individual rights also evolved.** California's CCPA is a later, scoped example of rights to know, delete with exceptions, and opt out of sale/sharing—not a 2015 rule or a universal US guarantee. ([California AG](https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa))

### A practical way to draw the line

| Proposed category                                             | Default approach                                    | Test to ask out loud                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Necessary service operations                                  | Allow bounded company discretion.                   | “Is this actually needed to provide what I requested?”      |
| Unnecessary sensitive profiling or unrelated reuse            | Require meaningful choice and strong limits.        | “Why should receiving the service mean accepting this too?” |
| Necessary evidence, required accounting, protected expression | Permit justified exceptions to deletion or refusal. | “What must remain, for what reason, and for how long?”      |
| Public-health or other justified research                     | Assess the applicable grounds and safeguards.       | “Can we achieve the benefit with less exposure?”            |

The distinction between lawful bases, purpose limits, and erasure exceptions has a legal illustration in [GDPR, Arts. 5–6, 9, and 17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng). The proposed ethical allocation of control is our argument, not a finding that one law settles every case.

## Use cases

### Use case 1: A navigation app and location history

- **Hypothetical:** An app collects location to give directions, then sells histories revealing visits to a clinic or place of worship.
- **Side A:** Argue that clear, voluntary terms can authorize downstream commercial decisions that fund the app. Enforce promises and general safety duties rather than giving each user a veto over every analysis or transfer.
- **Side B:** Directions don't require unlimited downstream sale. The person exposed should have meaningful notice and control.
- **Real-world anchor:** The FTC's final X-Mode/Outlogic settlement prohibited sharing or selling sensitive location data. The complaint alleged inadequate safeguards; don't turn alleged risks into a proven story about a particular victim. ([FTC final order announcement, 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-order-x-mode-successor-outlogic-prohibiting-it-sharing-or-selling-sensitive-location))

### Use case 2: A bank keeps records after an account closes

- **Hypothetical:** A customer requests deletion of transaction records, including evidence needed for a fraud investigation or legally required accounting retention.
- **Side A:** An unconditional deletion right would undermine fraud detection and reliable records.
- **Side B:** Keep only what the justified purpose requires, explain the reason, and don't treat required retention as permission to sell a spending profile.
- **Legal anchor:** Fraud prevention can support qualified legitimate interests; legal obligations and legal claims can defeat an erasure request where necessary. Neither creates a blanket exception for every use. ([GDPR, Art. 6, Art. 17(3), recital 47](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))

### Use case 3: A health-service company wants to train AI

- **Hypothetical:** A company collects a patient's messages to provide care, then proposes using them to train a general-purpose model.
- **Side A:** Argue for company-led reuse because future medical or service improvements aren't all predictable at collection. Clear training terms and accountable risk management can authorize decisions without repeated individual approval.
- **Side B:** Agreement to collection for care isn't automatically agreement to model training. Ask about the training purpose, legal basis, sensitive data, and exposure risks.
- **Evidence anchor:** FDA documents useful real-world evidence in regulatory decisions, but that doesn't prove unrestricted AI training is necessary. GPT-2 extraction research demonstrates possible memorization, not that every model reveals every patient record. ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/science-research/real-world-evidence/fda-use-real-world-evidence-regulatory-decision-making); [Carlini et al.](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/carlini-extracting))

## Positions

### Company control

I believe companies should control downstream decisions about data collected under clear, voluntarily accepted terms. People should be able to choose an exchange and delegate those decisions, just as they delegate other parts of a service. The company invests in the systems, security, and analysis that turn information into something useful. That investment deserves authority to develop the service and benefit from the value it creates.

I also think an individual veto is the wrong default for every future use. We can't predict every useful fraud pattern, research question, or service improvement when information is first collected. Let companies make those decisions and hold them responsible for deception and harm through enforceable promises and general safety duties. Control over copies and processing isn't ownership of a human being. It's authority to operate a service people chose to use.

#### Sources

- GDPR's effect on firm data use; data and computation as complements: [Demirer et al., NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32146).
- Useful reuse: [FDA real-world evidence](https://www.fda.gov/science-research/real-world-evidence/fda-use-real-world-evidence-regulatory-decision-making).
- Routine processing and fraud prevention: [GDPR, Art. 6 and recital 47](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng).

### Individual control

I think individuals should have the stronger claim because information about a person can affect that person's safety, opportunities, and freedom. Giving an app my location for directions doesn't mean I gave it permission to sell my clinic visits. Companies should explain relevant collection and reuse, let people challenge inaccurate profiles, and stop unnecessary retention or sharing.

I don't mean that I can erase fraud evidence, legally required accounts, or every news story mentioning me. I mean enforceable rights with narrow, justified exceptions. Companies can still operate and research can still happen. The difference is that collecting information doesn't automatically give the collector unlimited power over the person it describes.

#### Sources

- Context and deception: [FTC Cambridge Analytica finding](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/12/ftc-issues-opinion-order-against-cambridge-analytica-deceiving-consumers-about-collection-facebook).
- Sensitive exposure: [FTC X-Mode/Outlogic final settlement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-order-x-mode-successor-outlogic-prohibiting-it-sharing-or-selling-sensitive-location).
- Rights and their limits: [GDPR, Arts. 5–7 and 15–21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng).

## Debate prep

### The one idea to remember

**Who should decide what happens next?** Side A favors company authority delegated through meaningful agreements, backed by accountability for harm. Side B favors continuing individual rights over consequential uses. Both must explain how their approach preserves benefits and protects people.

### Side A: Company control

**Thesis:** As an ethical position, clear voluntary collection agreements should transfer downstream decision authority to the company: it creates value, coordinates beneficial reuse, and can be held accountable without granting every individual a continuing veto.

**Point 1: Investment and value creation warrant downstream control.**

- Example: Using seven years of data from a global cloud provider, researchers found that after GDPR, EU firms cut data storage by 26% and processing by 15% relative to comparable US firms—equivalent to a 22% rise in the cost of data. They also found data and computation are strong complements: information becomes valuable through the systems companies build around it. ([Demirer et al., NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32146))
- Say: “A record doesn't build a secure platform or discover a useful pattern by itself. If the company makes that investment, it should direct what it builds. Shifting control away from companies has a measurable cost: EU firms became less data-intensive after GDPR.”
- Idea behind it: an ethical claim about reciprocity and responsibility. People receive the agreed service; the company earns decision authority over the value-producing operation. This allocation is an argument, not the study's finding of ownership.

**Point 2: Unpredictable reuse and collective benefits argue against individual vetoes.**

- Example: Fraud detection works by spotting patterns across many customers' records—something no single customer can evaluate or approve, and which GDPR itself treats as a legitimate interest when strictly necessary. Similarly, FDA uses real-world evidence from records collected for care and billing in approvals and safety reviews, a purpose few patients anticipated at collection. ([GDPR, recital 47](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng); [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/science-research/real-world-evidence/fda-use-real-world-evidence-regulatory-decision-making))
- Say: “We can't list tomorrow's useful discoveries today. A personal veto over every later use can block benefits for people who weren't part of the original transaction.”
- Idea behind it: an ethical consequences argument. Coordinated company-led analysis can pursue shared benefits; require evidence of those benefits and accountability for risk, rather than automatic individual reapproval.

**Point 3: Meaningful agreements delegate decisions, not just permission to hold a record.**

- Example: GDPR recognizes necessary contract processing and qualified legitimate interests, alongside specific, informed consent. This legal example distinguishes permission for processing from consent to every operation; it doesn't itself establish the proposed ethical delegation. ([GDPR, Arts. 4(11), 6–7](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))
- Say: “Respecting my choice includes respecting my choice to delegate. If the terms clearly authorize downstream uses, the company should be able to rely on that agreement.”
- Idea behind it: an ethical autonomy and reliability argument. A voluntary exchange can assign decision authority within an understood range; opaque boilerplate can't do the work of meaningful authorization.

**Weak spot / concession:** The evidence supports broad discretion, not an unlimited legal right or a factual finding that collection transfers ownership. Accountability for harm narrows literal absolutism. My ethical case still needs to show that agreements are genuinely voluntary and that general duties protect people effectively without continuing individual vetoes.

**If they say... → I say...**

- “It's my data, so I should control every use.” → “It describes you, but the company builds and operates the processing system. Control over those copies and operations isn't ownership of you; it's a division of authority in an agreed exchange.”
- “Every secondary use needs fresh consent.” → “Meaningful terms can authorize a range of downstream uses in advance. People can delegate decisions; reopening each one isn't the only way to respect their choice.”
- “A privacy promise doesn't justify an unrelated sale.” → “Enforce the promise. My position depends on honoring the authorization actually given, not inventing it afterward. Broken promises call for remedies, not a veto over every honestly authorized use.”
- “Research doesn't require company-led control.” → “Fraud detection shows otherwise: a pattern across thousands of accounts only appears to whoever holds all of them. If each person could withdraw their record, the pattern disappears. Someone has to be authorized to look across the whole set.”
- “Companies make money while people take the risk.” → “Make companies answer for the risks they impose through enforceable safety duties, oversight, and remedies. Commercial discretion and responsibility can go together without subject-by-subject prior control.”
- “If you accept safeguards, you've conceded ‘whatever they wish’ is too strong.” → “No. ‘Whatever they wish’ means discretion within what the person agreed to—not a license to break the agreement or cause unlawful harm. Every right, including yours, operates within law.”
- “Your accountability approach still permits harmful decisions.” → “So can an individual-choice system: people can't assess every buyer or model. Company-led responsibility puts the duty on the organization with the expertise and capacity to manage those risks.”

**Closing line:** “Honor meaningful agreements, let companies direct the value they build, and hold them responsible for harm. Company-led control keeps services workable and leaves room for discoveries an individual veto can stop before anyone sees the benefit.”

### Side B: Individual control

**Thesis:** People should have meaningful knowledge and enforceable control over consequential information about them, with necessary, proportionate exceptions—not absolute property rights over every record.

**Point 1: Sharing for one purpose isn't consent to every purpose.**

- Example: The FTC found Cambridge Analytica deceptively harvested Facebook information for voter profiling and targeting, including information about app users' friends. This establishes deception, not that its work changed an election result. ([FTC finding, 2019](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/12/ftc-issues-opinion-order-against-cambridge-analytica-deceiving-consumers-about-collection-facebook))
- Say: “I can agree to a service without agreeing to every future use of my identity—and my friend can't simply agree for me.”
- Idea behind it: autonomy and context. A person's meaningful choice depends on what was actually explained.

**Point 2: The person bears harms the seller doesn't bear.**

- Example: The FTC's X-Mode/Outlogic settlement restricted sensitive location sales following allegations about data revealing clinic and worship visits. It documents an enforcement response to exposure risks, not a proven downstream injury to a named person. ([FTC final settlement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-finalizes-order-x-mode-successor-outlogic-prohibiting-it-sharing-or-selling-sensitive-location))
- Say: “The company receives the sale price. I live with what the buyer learns about me. My interests can't disappear at collection.”
- Idea behind it: fairness and prevention. Profit shouldn't depend on imposing unchosen exposure on someone else.

**Point 3: Protection must follow inferences and model exposure too.**

- Example: Facebook Likes research demonstrated sensitive-trait prediction in a volunteer sample. GPT-2 research extracted some verbatim training material, including personal contact information. These show possibilities under particular conditions, not universal accuracy or leakage. ([Kosinski et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625324/); [Carlini et al.](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/carlini-extracting))
- Say: “Calling it an inference or putting it in a model doesn't make it stop being information about a person.”
- Idea behind it: accountability follows the risk, not just the original database row. Inferred profiles also need routes to challenge error.

**Weak spot / concession:** Literal control over whether any personal data exists would let people erase evidence or suppress others' expression. Accept necessary fraud evidence and accounting retention, free-expression protection, and justified public-health uses. Exceptions need reasons, scope, and review—not a permanent blank check. ([GDPR, recital 4 and Art. 17(3)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))

**If they say... → I say...**

- “You clicked agree.” → “What consequential use was honestly explained? Clear permission can matter; deception and unknown future uses don't become ethical through a click.”
- “Your approach would stop medical research.” → “Not if justified research has proportionate exceptions and safeguards. Show why this data and use are necessary.”
- “A fraudster could demand deletion.” → “Necessary evidence can remain. A right with exceptions isn't the same as having no rights.”
- “We anonymized it.” → “Genuinely anonymous data is different. Removing names alone isn't proof: researchers linked Netflix records using outside information.” ([Narayanan and Shmatikov](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0610105))
- “GDPR made data more expensive for firms.” → “The same study found that's only a 0.1–0.6% rise in overall production costs. Protection didn't stop companies from operating.” ([Demirer et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32146))
- “We inferred it ourselves, so it's ours.” → “Your work explains how you produced the profile, not why selling a sensitive claim about me is justified.”
- “Deletion is technically impossible after training.” → “Then explain what can be removed and what remains. Difficulty calls for honest remedies and better training choices, not unlimited permission.”

**Closing line:** “Companies can use data without owning the person behind it. Individual rights with justified exceptions protect useful services and prevent collection from becoming unchecked power.”

### AI-specific reminders

- **Collection versus training:** Permission or another lawful basis for one service isn't automatic permission for a different training purpose. Identify the purpose and applicable basis; don't claim every training use legally requires consent. ([GDPR, Arts. 5–6 and 9](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng))
- **Inferred data:** A prediction about health, politics, or sexuality can matter even if the person never supplied that trait. Predictions can be wrong; the Likes study doesn't establish reliable truth about everyone. ([Original inference study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625324/))
- **Memorization:** Some models under some attack conditions reproduce training sequences. Don't say all models store searchable copies of every record. ([Original extraction study](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/carlini-extracting))
- **Deletion isn't instant untraining:** Removing source data and addressing learned model exposure are different targets. Ask what retraining, unlearning, or exposure controls were verified; don't promise any method removes every trace. The extraction study demonstrates exposure, not a universal deletion solution. ([Carlini et al.](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/carlini-extracting))

### Keep the disagreement practical

Use the shared cases to test **accountability for harm versus prior individual control**, not just competing slogans about ownership.

- **Side A asks:** “Why should someone who voluntarily authorized this range of uses retain a veto over each company decision?”
- **Side B asks:** “What makes authorization meaningful when the person can't foresee the consequential uses?”
- **Side A must explain:** Who enforces promises and safety duties, what remedies exist, and how foreseeable harms are prevented—not just compensated afterward.
- **Side B must explain:** Which uses people can refuse and which necessary uses continue despite objection.

**Useful line for either side:** “Which system gives the decision to the right party and makes that party answer for the consequences?”

### Debate portion

**Opening (about 1 minute):** State your thesis, name the three points in one sentence each, and give one shared example. Side A emphasizes delegated authority, investment, and future benefits; Side B emphasizes context, exposure, and continuing rights.

**The three places the argument actually turns:**

1. **Does collection transfer authority?** Side A: investment and genuine agreements warrant discretion. Side B: limited disclosure doesn't surrender continuing rights.
2. **What happens when choices conflict?** Side A: an absolute veto blocks needed evidence and beneficial reuse. Side B: justified exceptions needn't authorize unrelated profiling or sale.
3. **Accountability or prior control?** Side A: enforce agreements and general safety duties while companies decide how to use data. Side B: the collector should justify consequential uses before imposing risks that later remedies can't undo.

**Questions to ask the company side:**

- “What step turns permission to collect into permission for every later use?”
- “Does your literal position allow selling clinic visits despite an explicit promise not to?”
- “If both parties create value, why should only the company control the result?”
- “What benefit requires unlimited rights rather than permission for a specific justified use?”
- “If your answer adds safeguards, are you conceding that ‘whatever they wish’ is too strong?”

**Questions to ask the individual side:**

- “Should someone be able to erase fraud evidence or legally required accounts? Where is your limit?”
- “When should public-health research proceed despite an individual's objection?”
- “Can I delete another person's truthful reporting about me? How do you protect expression?”
- “What would make a voluntary agreement to broader reuse meaningful rather than coercive?”
- “What evidence would show a deletion remedy addressed both source records and model exposure?”

**If asked, ‘Which approach is better?’**

_Use this only when asked for your own judgment outside an assigned side. If you're arguing Side A, stick to the Side A closing line._

I favor **individual-led stewardship**: meaningful notice, purpose limits, access and correction, control over unnecessary uses, and accountable retention and deletion. Companies retain justified operational discretion, and necessary evidence, accounting, expression, and public-health uses receive narrow protection. This isn't property absolutism or consent for every operation. It's a reasoned ethical judgment: the person bears consequences that company investment and initial collection don't erase. The literal unlimited company position fails that test; literal unlimited individual control also goes too far.

**If you get stuck:** “Who should decide this use, what authorizes that decision, and who answers for its consequences?”
