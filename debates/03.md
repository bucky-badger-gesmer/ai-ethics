# Debate 03 — AI Traffic Court for the City of Omaha

**Course:** SEIS 651: AI Ethics · University of St. Thomas

|                |                                                                                |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Question**   | Should Omaha replace judge-led traffic court hearings with an AI kiosk system? |
| **Position 1** | Support: consultants advising the City of Omaha                                |
| **Position 2** | Oppose: community members against automating traffic court                     |

## Scenario

_(Fictional.)_ Omaha wants to cut the time and money spent on due process for traffic violations. The proposal replaces judge-led hearings with an AI system. Five kiosks go in the current traffic courtroom, so five cases run at once. The defendant states their case and submits evidence at a kiosk. The AI reviews it against whatever else is in its database and gives a verdict in about a minute. The system is still being designed.

## Preparation

Consider the differences between **ethics and morality**, the **benefits/costs of efficiency**, impacts on **quality of life**, and impacts on **vulnerable populations**.

## Positions

### Support (consultants)

I believe an AI traffic court can give people a fairer and faster hearing than they get today. Traffic court is high volume and low stakes compared to criminal court. Most hearings already take a few minutes, and people lose half a day or more in the courtroom. Due process has never meant "a judge in a robe no matter what." The Supreme Court says it depends on the stakes, the risk of error, and the cost to the government (_Mathews v. Eldridge_). Human judges are also inconsistent. Studies have found rulings shift with things like time since a meal break or a local team's loss. A system gives the same facts the same result.

I think the ethical case depends on how it's built, so I'd make these conditions part of the proposal. Anyone can ask for a human judge instead, or appeal to one. Every verdict comes with written reasons. The kiosks work in multiple languages and with accessibility options. The city runs an outside audit for bias by race, language, and neighborhood, and publishes the results. With those in place, the time and money saved go back to the people who can least afford to lose a day of work.

- _Mathews v. Eldridge_, 424 U.S. 319 (1976): https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/424/319/
- Danziger et al., 2011, "Extraneous factors in judicial decisions": https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1018033108
- Eren & Mocan, 2018, "Emotional Judges and Unlucky Juveniles" (American Economic Journal: Applied Economics)
- The Belmont Report: https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/belmont-report/read-the-belmont-report/index.html

### Oppose (community members)

I believe automating traffic court takes away the part of due process that matters most: being heard by someone who can understand you and use judgment. A kiosk that listens to you talk and rules in a minute can't do that. Speech recognition makes almost twice as many errors for Black speakers as for white speakers. It struggles with accents, limited English, and speech disabilities. In a city with large Spanish-speaking and refugee communities, those are the people who'd be misheard. And the AI checks "other relevant information in its database." That means prior stops and records, which reflect where police patrol, not who drives worse.

I also think the stakes are bigger than they look. In Nebraska, 12 points in two years costs you your license. For someone who drives to work, that means losing the job, and unpaid fines pile up from there. Governments that automated decisions like this, such as Michigan's unemployment fraud system and Australia's Robodebt, wrongly accused thousands of people. It took years to undo. A human judge can reduce a fine, set a payment plan, or dismiss a ticket because you fixed the taillight. The city calls that inefficiency. For many people, it's the only mercy they'll get in the system.

## Debate prep

### The one idea to remember

Both sides agree traffic court should be **fair, fast, and affordable**. The debate is about what fairness requires: **consistent outcomes** (support), or **being heard by a person with judgment** (oppose). It also turns on who pays when the system is wrong.

### Side A: Support (consultants)

**Thesis:** Due process means a fair, timely, reviewable hearing, not necessarily a human one. For high-volume, low-stakes traffic cases, a well-governed AI system can be more consistent, more accessible, and less costly for the people who use it.

**Point 1: Consistency is fairness. Human judges aren't consistent.**

- Example: In the Israeli parole study, favorable rulings dropped from about 65% to near zero right before a judge's meal break, then jumped back after. (The study is debated, but the broader finding that irrelevant factors sway judges holds up.)
- Example: Louisiana juvenile judges gave harsher sentences the week after an unexpected LSU football loss (Eren & Mocan), and Black defendants took most of the impact.
- Say: "Same facts, same verdict, regardless of who you are or what time you show up. That's the promise of equal justice, and humans don't keep it."
- Idea behind it: ethics (Dewey) is reasoned evaluation. It doesn't protect a custom because it's familiar. Belmont justice means equal treatment for equal cases.

**Point 2: Efficiency is a quality-of-life issue.**

- Example: A traffic hearing can mean taking unpaid time off, arranging childcare, paying for parking, and waiting hours for a few minutes before a judge. Five kiosks at a minute each cut the wait down to almost nothing.
- Example: Judges and clerks freed from routine traffic cases can take on serious cases, like protection orders and evictions, that are backlogged.
- Say: "The person hurt most by slow traffic court is an hourly worker who loses a shift to contest a $150 ticket. Speed is a benefit to them, not only to the city budget."
- Idea behind it: Aristotle's eudaimonia. Money and time are part of a good life. Beneficence means maximizing benefit.

**Point 3: It can be designed to protect vulnerable people better than the current courtroom.**

- Example: A kiosk can run in Spanish, Karen, Arabic, or any language, all day, with no wait for an interpreter. It can offer large text, audio, and sign-language video.
- Example: Some people are intimidated by a courtroom and a judge. A kiosk is less intimidating, so more people may contest instead of just paying.
- Say: "The question isn't whether the AI is perfect. It's whether it's better than a rushed hearing with no interpreter at 8 a.m."
- Idea behind it: Belmont justice (fair distribution of benefits and burdens). Arendt's voluntary associations suggest community oversight boards.

**Safeguards (the conditions that make it ethical):**

1. **Opt out to a human judge** at any point, and a right to appeal to one.
2. **Written reasons** for every verdict, so the defendant knows why and can challenge it.
3. **Independent bias audits** by race, language, disability, and ZIP code, with results made public.
4. **Limited data:** the AI sees the citation and the defendant's evidence, not unrelated police history.
5. **Pilot first** on one violation type (for example, parking or equipment violations) before expanding. That's Dewey's "definition at the end of the inquiry."

**More examples to pull from:**

- **Online dispute resolution:** British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal handles small claims and strata disputes online, with humans making the final calls. It shows courts can move online without losing legitimacy.
- **Automated enforcement already exists:** Red-light and speed cameras issue tickets with no officer present. Ruling on them at a kiosk is a smaller step than issuing them.
- **Hangzhou Internet Court (China, 2017):** Handles e-commerce and online disputes digitally at high volume. Use carefully. It shows scale is possible, but the other side will point to China's lack of judicial independence.

**If they say... → I say...**

- "A machine can't give due process." → "Due process is flexible by law. _Mathews v. Eldridge_ weighs the stakes, the risk of error, and the cost. For a speeding ticket, with a right to appeal to a human, this passes."
- "Speech recognition is biased." → "Then fix the input, not the whole idea. Offer typing, a human interpreter, or a human judge. And human judges have well-documented biases too."
- "Look at Robodebt and Michigan." → "Those systems had no human review and put the burden on citizens to prove innocence. Our proposal requires both human review and written reasons."
- "Judges show mercy." → "Put that discretion into the rules: automatic payment plans, and dismissal when the defendant shows the problem was fixed. Mercy that depends on which judge you get isn't fair."

**Closing line:** "The status quo isn't a wise judge with unlimited time. It's a crowded courtroom, a long wait, and a verdict that depends on who you draw. A transparent, audited system with a human backstop is the more ethical choice."

### Side B: Oppose (community members)

**Thesis:** Due process means being heard and judged by someone who can understand you and be held responsible. A one-minute kiosk replaces judgment with processing. Its errors will land on the people least able to fight back.

**Point 1: Being "heard" requires being understood.**

- Example: Koenecke et al. tested five major speech recognition systems. Average word error rate was 0.35 for Black speakers and 0.19 for white speakers, almost double.
- Example: Omaha's Spanish speakers, refugee communities, elderly residents, and people with speech or cognitive disabilities are the people a voice kiosk will mishear. Low-literacy defendants struggle to submit written evidence.
- Say: "If the machine can't understand you, you haven't been heard. You've just been recorded."
- Idea behind it: Belmont respect for persons. Dewey says ethics is choice "as affected by the rights of others."

**Point 2: Efficiency makes errors happen faster, and at scale.**

- Example: **Michigan MiDAS (2013–2015):** An automated unemployment system accused about 40,000 people of fraud. A later review found a 93% error rate in cases decided without human review. The state settled for $20 million.
- Example: **Australia Robodebt:** An automated system sent unlawful debt notices to hundreds of thousands of people. A Royal Commission called it "crude and cruel," and the government paid about A$1.8 billion in settlements.
- Example: **Dutch childcare benefits scandal:** A risk algorithm wrongly flagged about 26,000 families as fraudsters, many of them dual nationals. The government resigned in 2021.
- Say: "A human judge makes one mistake at a time. This system can make the same mistake five times a minute."
- Idea behind it: Bonhoeffer says judgment grows dull "before the sheen of success." Véliz says digital things spread at scale.

**Point 3: Traffic court isn't low stakes for vulnerable people, and judgment is where mercy lives.**

- Example: Nebraska revokes a license at 12 points in two years. Losing a license can mean losing a job, which leads to more unpaid fines, which leads to more suspensions. The DOJ's Ferguson report showed how traffic fines, used as revenue, trapped poor and Black residents in debt.
- Example: The AI uses "other relevant information in its database." Prior stops reflect where police patrol. ProPublica found that COMPAS falsely flagged Black defendants as high risk at almost twice the rate of white defendants.
- Example: In _State v. Loomis_, the defendant couldn't see how the proprietary COMPAS score was calculated. You can't cross-examine an algorithm.
- Say: "A judge can look at a single mom with a broken taillight and say, 'Fix it, case dismissed.' A kiosk just applies the rule."
- Idea behind it: Arendt's banality of evil. Harm happens when nobody has to think or take responsibility. And morals (customs of mercy, being treated as a person) are part of why people see courts as legitimate.

**More examples to pull from:**

- **DoNotPay "robot lawyer" (2023):** It planned to coach a defendant in traffic court through an earpiece and backed out after state bars warned about unauthorized practice of law. The FTC later fined the company $193,000 for claims it couldn't back up. If AI can't even be a traffic lawyer, why should it be the judge?
- **Estonia "robot judge" myth:** Widely reported in 2019 as an AI deciding small claims. Estonia's Ministry of Justice later said no such project existed. The hype runs ahead of reality.
- **EU AI Act:** Lists AI used in the administration of justice as **high-risk** (Annex III), with requirements for human oversight. Other governments see this as dangerous.
- **Procedural justice research (Tom Tyler):** People obey the law more when they feel they were treated fairly and heard, even when they lose. Taking the human out erodes trust in the courts.

**If they say... → I say...**

- "Human judges are biased too." → "Yes, but a biased judge can be appealed, reassigned, voted out, or reported. Who do you vote out when the vendor's model is wrong?"
- "There's a right to appeal to a human." → "The people most likely to be misjudged are the least likely to appeal. They have less time, less English, and more fear of the system. And if everyone appeals, you've saved nothing."
- "Due process is flexible (_Mathews_)." → "_Mathews_ also weighs the risk of error. A system with documented error gaps by race and accent fails that test."
- "It's faster and cheaper." → "Cheaper for the city. The cost moves to the wrongly convicted person, who loses points, pays a fine, or loses a license. Robodebt was cheap too, until it cost A$1.8 billion."
- "We'll audit it." → "Audits find harm after it happens. The system is still being designed, and you're already asking us to replace the judges."

**Closing line:** "Justice isn't only getting the right answer. It's being heard by someone who can be held responsible. A one-minute kiosk gives Omaha a faster verdict, not a fairer one, and the people it gets wrong will be the ones who can least afford it."

### Debate portion

**Opening (about 1 minute):** Thesis, then name all three points in one sentence each, then one example.

**The three places the argument actually turns:**

1. **What's the baseline?** Support: a rushed, inconsistent courtroom. Oppose: a human judge who can use judgment and mercy.
2. **Replacement or first step?** Support: AI decides, but a human is always available. Oppose: the scenario says _replace_, and appeal rights don't help people who won't use them.
3. **Who pays for the errors?** Support: errors can be audited and fixed system-wide. Oppose: errors land on the vulnerable, at scale, before anyone notices.

**How each prep topic maps to the points:**

| Prep topic                | Support                                            | Oppose                                                       |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Ethics vs. morality       | Reasoned fairness beats inherited custom (Pt 1)    | Custom of being heard is what makes courts legitimate (Pt 3) |
| Efficiency benefits/costs | Time and money back to defendants (Pt 2)           | Errors happen faster and at scale (Pt 2)                     |
| Quality of life           | No lost shifts, childcare, or waits (Pt 2)         | License loss, lost jobs, debt spirals (Pt 3)                 |
| Vulnerable populations    | Multilingual, accessible, less intimidating (Pt 3) | Misheard, over-flagged, least able to appeal (Pts 1 & 3)     |

**Questions to ask the support side:**

- "What happens when the kiosk mishears a defendant with a heavy accent? How would anyone know?"
- "What's in the 'other relevant information in its database,' and who decided that?"
- "If the verdict is wrong, who's accountable: the city, the vendor, or the clerk?"
- "If most people appeal to a human, where are the savings? If most don't, how do you know the verdicts were right?"

**Questions to ask the oppose side:**

- "Is the current system fair to an hourly worker who loses a day's pay to contest a ticket?"
- "If a judge's ruling depends on whether they've had lunch, is that the human judgment you want to protect?"
- "Would you support it with a guaranteed opt-out to a human judge? If not, why not?"
- "Red-light cameras already issue tickets with no human. Why is ruling on them different?"

**If you get stuck:** Go back to the one idea: "What does fairness require, and who pays when the system gets it wrong?" It works for either side.
