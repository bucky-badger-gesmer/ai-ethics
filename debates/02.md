# Debate 02 — Synthetic Data for Training AI Models

**Course:** SEIS 651: AI Ethics · University of St. Thomas

|                |                                                         |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Question**   | Is it ethical to use synthetic data to train AI models? |
| **Position 1** | Mostly ethical                                          |
| **Position 2** | Mostly unethical                                        |

## Preparation

Identify **one or two use cases** for synthetic data to use as examples in the debate.

Consider how these positions may be shaped by accuracy, privacy, bias, cost, governance, model output, etc., and build on this discussion using any other relevant factors not covered in class.

---

## Use cases

### Use case 1

- Context: Frid-Adar et al. had 182 liver CT scans and used a GAN to make more. Sensitivity went from 78.6% to 85.7%, and specificity from 88.4% to 92.4%.
- Why relevant: Rare medical images are hard to collect, and the synthetic ones improved the model without scanning more patients.

### Use case 2

- Context: Alemohammad et al. retrained generative models on their own output. Without fresh real data each round, quality or diversity drops. They call that Model Autophagy Disorder.
- Why relevant: This is what happens when synthetic data replaces real data over time.

## Positions

### Mostly ethical

I believe synthetic data is mostly ethical when it fills a gap you can't fill with real data. In the liver study, they didn't have enough CT scans, and getting more meant more patients. The synthetic images made the classifier more accurate. It's also better for privacy, since a synthetic record isn't someone's chart, and it can fill in groups the real sample missed, like faces that never showed up in a facial-recognition set.

I also think it's the practical choice. It's cheap, and a small team can't always buy a huge labeled set. The condition I'd keep is the one from Alemohammad: real data still has to stay in the mix, and you should say when data is synthetic. Under that, I think the accuracy and privacy gain is worth it.

#### Sources

- Frid-Adar et al., 2018: https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01229
- Alemohammad et al., 2023: https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01850
- Gebru et al., Datasheets for Datasets: https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09010

### Mostly unethical

I believe synthetic data is mostly unethical because you need real data to make it, and that real data brings the original problems with it. If the generator was trained on scraped or biased data, the synthetic set inherits that. Calling it "not human subjects" doesn't fix consent, copyright, or bias.

I think the harm shows up in the model. Alemohammad found that training on synthetic data over and over makes quality or diversity fall unless you keep adding fresh real data. And when the real record is already unfair, like hiring or loans, a "balanced" synthetic set trains the model on a world that isn't real. The liver study is a narrow case. The normal case is the next model trained on the last model's output.

## Debate prep

### The one idea to remember

Both sides agree that synthetic data is only as good as the real data behind it. The debate is about whether that's a **limit you can manage** (ethical) or a **flaw you can't escape** (unethical).

### Side A: Mostly ethical

**Thesis:** Synthetic data is mostly ethical when it fills a gap real data can't, protects people's privacy, and is still checked against the real world.

**Point 1: It helps when real data is scarce.**

- Example: The liver study had only 182 lesions. Synthetic images raised sensitivity from 78.6% to 85.7%, so about 7 more out of every 100 real lesions got caught.
- Say: "Refusing synthetic data here doesn't protect anyone. It just means more missed diagnoses."
- Idea behind it: judge an action by its consequences. If it prevents harm and costs nobody anything, you should do it.

**Point 2: It protects privacy better than "anonymized" real data.**

- Example: Anonymized data gets re-identified all the time. Most Americans can be singled out by ZIP code, birth date, and sex alone. A synthetic record isn't anyone's chart.
- Say: "Every real record is a real person. Synthetic data lets us learn from a population without using any one person."
- Idea behind it: respect for persons. Use as few people's data as you can.

**Point 3: It can fill in groups that real data leaves out.**

- Example: Facial recognition has worked worst for darker-skinned women because they were missing from the training data. Synthetic data can help fill that gap.
- Say: "If you didn't know which group you'd be born into, you'd want the model fixed for the group it fails."
- Idea behind it: fairness means improving things for the worst-off.

**More examples to pull from:**

- **Self-driving cars (Waymo):** Billions of simulated miles, including crashes and jaywalkers that would be dangerous or impossible to stage. "You can't ethically stage a child running into the street."
- **Content moderation:** Synthetic abusive text trains safety filters. Human labelers, such as Kenyan contractors paid under $2/hour to label graphic content for OpenAI, suffered trauma doing that work. Synthetic data spares people that work.
- **Synthea (MITRE):** Fake but realistic patient records for building and testing health software, so developers never touch a real chart.
- **AlphaFold and robotics:** AlphaFold 2 trained partly on its own predicted protein structures, and OpenAI trained a robot hand in randomized simulations. Both were then checked against the real world, which is "train synthetic, test real" working.

**My conditions (these are why it's "mostly"):**

1. Train on synthetic data, but **test on real data**.
2. Keep real data in the mix, so models don't collapse.
3. **Label** synthetic data as synthetic.

**If they say... → I say...**

- "It's just copying the biases of the generator." → "That's why you test on real data. If real-world errors go down, it worked."
- "Model collapse proves it's dangerous." → "Collapse happens when you _replace_ real data. I'm arguing for _adding to_ it."
- "Nobody consented to the generator's training data." → "That's a problem with how the generator was built, not with synthetic data itself. Real datasets have the same consent problem, and it's worse there."
- "The liver study is too small to prove anything." → "Then test it on more real data. That's an argument for checking the results, not for banning the method."

**Quantum computing angle:** Quantum computers will eventually break today's encryption, so stored real data becomes riskier to keep. Synthetic data you generate doesn't have that risk, because there's no real person's data in it to steal.

**Closing line:** "The alternative to synthetic data usually isn't better real data. It's no data, or data taken from people who never agreed. Used with real testing and honest labeling, synthetic data is the more ethical choice."

### Side B: Mostly unethical

**Thesis:** Synthetic data is mostly unethical because it can't create new knowledge. It repackages real data's problems (bias, consent, copyright) and makes them harder to see.

**Point 1: You can't make up evidence.**

- Example: The liver study started with 182 lesions and still only has 182 lesions' worth of information. The rare lesion the generator never saw is exactly the one the model will miss, and the better score hides that.
- Say: "Synthetic data is the generator's guesses dressed up as observations."
- Idea behind it: processing data can't add information that wasn't already there.

**Point 2: It hides consent problems.**

- Example: A patient shares a scan for their own care, not to train a company's generator. Once the data is synthetic, it's "nobody's data," so nobody can object.
- Say: "Synthetic data doesn't solve consent. It just makes consent problems impossible to see."
- Idea behind it: privacy is about information going where people expected it to go, not only about keeping names hidden.

**Point 3: It fakes fairness and pollutes future data.**

- Example: In hiring or lending, a "balanced" synthetic dataset trains the model on a world that doesn't exist, and then the model is used in the world that does.
- Example: Alemohammad showed that models trained on their own output lose quality or diversity each round. As the internet fills with AI-made content, every future model gets worse.
- Say: "If you don't have real data for a group, you can't check whether the synthetic data for them is accurate."
- Idea behind it: what's cheap for one company becomes costly for everyone.

**More examples to pull from:**

- **Stanford Alpaca:** Trained on about 52,000 answers from an OpenAI model, even though OpenAI's terms forbade using its output to build competing models. One company's output became another's training data. That's consent laundering in practice.
- **U.S. Census:** Released synthetic versions of survey and business data, and added privacy noise to the 2020 Census. Critics said small towns and tribal areas got distorted counts, and those counts affect funding and redistricting. The privacy protection hit the smallest groups hardest.
- **Microsoft Phi models:** Small language models trained heavily on AI-written "textbook" data. It works for now, but it's exactly the "next model trained on the last model's output" path toward collapse.
- **AI feedback for safety training (Anthropic's Constitutional AI):** A model critiques and labels its own answers. It's cheap, but the AI grades its own homework. Whose values end up in the labels?

**If they say... → I say...**

- "Just test on real data." → "If you had enough real data to test fairly, you'd often have enough to train on. The group you're filling in is exactly the group you can't test."
- "It protects privacy." → "Research shows synthetic data can still leak personal information. And hiding individuals doesn't stop harm to a whole group."
- "Rotating an image is synthetic too." → "Rotating an image doesn't make anything up, since a rotated tumor is still a tumor. A generator invents details nobody has checked."
- "The alternative is no data." → "Then the ethical answer might be to collect real data with consent, not to fake it."

**Quantum computing angle:** Quantum computing makes synthetic data more accurate only for physics and chemistry, where it's built from laws of nature. For data about people, more computing power just means more convincing guesses.

**Closing line:** "The best case for synthetic data is a narrow medical study. The normal case is the next model trained on the last model's output. Judge it by how it's actually used, and it's mostly unethical."

### Debate portion

**Opening (about 1 minute):** Thesis, then name all three points in one sentence each, then one example.

**The three places the argument actually turns:**

1. **What's the alternative?** Ethical side: no data at all. Unethical side: real data nobody bothered to collect.
2. **Does it add knowledge or only guesses?** Ethical side: the real-world results got better. Unethical side: the benchmark improved, but the rare cases disappeared.
3. **Judge the best use or the typical use?** Ethical side: judge it with safeguards. Unethical side: judge it by what companies actually do.

**Questions to ask the ethical side:**

- "If the generator never saw a rare case, how can its synthetic data teach the model about it?"
- "How do you test fairness for a group you had to fake because you didn't have real data?"
- "Who consented to the data the generator learned from?"

**Questions to ask the unethical side:**

- "For a rare disease with only 182 cases, what's your ethical alternative? Wait for more patients to get sick?"
- "Is rotating or flipping an image unethical? Where's the line?"
- "If synthetic data reveals less about each person than the real data does, who exactly is harmed?"

**If you get stuck:** Go back to the one idea. "Everything depends on the real data behind it." It works for either side.
