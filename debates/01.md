| **Question**   | Should the term _"artificial intelligence"_ be defined **broadly** or **narrowly**? |

## Preparation

Draft your own **broad** and **narrow** definitions of _"artificial intelligence"_ to share during the debate.

Consider how **audience** (e.g., policy makers, researchers, practitioners) and **implications** for regulation, innovation, and ethical oversight/governance shape each position.

---

## Working definitions

### Broad

I believe that AI is any machine that makes choices without a person telling it what to do each time. That covers Netflix recommendations, email spam filters, and even a thermostat turning on the heat.

I also believe that this definition helps policy makers best. For regulation, it's hard to dodge; a company can't escape oversight by claiming its system "doesn't really learn," and the definition doesn't go stale when the technology changes. For ethics, it's the safest choice, because harms like biased hiring decisions come from automated decision-making in general, not just from advanced models. For innovation, it creates a level playing field, where everyone follows the same rules, so nobody gains an edge by relabeling their product as "just software."

### Narrow

I believe that AI is a machine that can learn and figure out things nobody taught it. That includes systems like ChatGPT that improve from data, but excludes a basic thermostat or a rule-based spam filter.

I think this definition works best for researchers and for people actually building this stuff. If we call everything AI, then the word doesn't mean anything anymore. It also helps with regulation, because the rules end up aimed at the systems that are actually hard to predict, not at a spam filter. And it's better for innovation. If I build a scheduling tool, I shouldn't have to go through the same review process as someone building the next ChatGPT. Same thing for ethics, where the real concern is systems we can't fully explain, so that's where the attention should go.
