## Quick card

## Scenario

Some organizations offer the IAT to employees as a resource; some **require** it, with or without disclosure of results, aiming to identify and begin reducing social bias.

Explore the [Project Implicit](https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/takeatest.html) site (taking a test is optional) and watch the [background video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=11Jg97_dkXM).

Consider the **limitations** of the test and whether **other tools** may be more appropriate. Consider the perspectives of the **test taker**, the **organization**, and **stakeholders or members of the public** affected by their work.

**Framing reminders:**

- The question has three different uses hidden in it: **offered** (voluntary, private), **required but private**, and **required with disclosed results**. Most of the ethical difference lives here. Name which one you're defending.
- "Assess" is the key word. Project Implicit itself separates **education** (fine) from **diagnosis/selection** (not fine). Side A should defend education; Side B should hold Side A to the word "assess."
- Week 8 draws the same line for models: "Are we using this model to learn about the training data, or to create/predict something that will impact a person's life or livelihood?" Learning is Side A's ground; impact on a person's livelihood is Side B's.
- This is a bias **measurement** question, the same one Week 8 raises for models: what is being measured, how accurately, and what is the impact on people?

## Evidence on the IAT

| Claim                         | What the evidence says                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reliability**               | Average test-retest r = .50 across 58 studies (Greenwald & Lai, 2020); others report .44 (Gawronski et al., 2017). Internal consistency is higher (α ≈ .80). Averaging repeated tests can reach r ≈ .89.                                                          |
| **Diagnostic use**            | Greenwald et al. (2022): r = .50 "is not adequate to justify treating a single IAT observation as accurately diagnostic of an individual." Project Implicit: it "does not meet the standards of measurement reliability for diagnostic use."                      |
| **Predicting discrimination** | Oswald et al. (2013): "IATs were poor predictors of every criterion category other than brain activity, and the IATs performed no better than simple explicit measures."                                                                                          |
| **Greenwald's response**      | Greenwald, Banaji & Nosek (2015): statistically small effects can have societally large effects when applied across many people and decisions. Oswald et al. (2015) reply that these thought experiments rely on "untested and, arguably, untenable assumptions." |
| **Changing bias**             | Forscher et al. (2019), 492 studies: implicit measures can change, but effects are weak, behavior changes were "generally trivial," and "changes in implicit measures did not mediate changes in explicit measures or behavior."                                  |
| **Ethics policy**             | Project Implicit: participation "should not be mandatory," "never ask anyone to share their personal feedback," don't use for "hiring or qualification decisions," but it's "an effective educational tool for raising awareness."                                |

**Other tools that may be more appropriate:** adverse impact analysis of actual outcomes (Week 8 references), structured interviews and rubrics, blind or anonymized review, process audits, and anonymous group-level IAT use in training rather than individual assessment.

## Positions

### Mostly ethical

I believe implicit bias testing is mostly ethical when it's used the way its creators intend: as a voluntary, private, educational tool. Week 8 reminds us that "we are all 'biased' in some way" and that we should "continually assess your own biases." The IAT is one of the few ways to see a bias you don't consciously hold. Its creators acknowledge it can't diagnose individuals, but at the group level its effects are real, and Greenwald, Banaji and Nosek argue that small effects repeated across many decisions add up to large societal harm.

The ethical version has clear rules, and Project Implicit already wrote them: offer it, don't require it; never ask for anyone's results; never use it for hiring or evaluation; and pair it with real process changes. Under those conditions, it's a low-cost, low-risk way to start the conversation.

#### Sources

- Project Implicit, Ethical Considerations: https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/ethics.html
- Greenwald, Banaji & Nosek, 2015, "Statistically small effects of the IAT can have societally large effects" (JPSP 108(4)): https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000016
- Greenwald et al., 2022, "Best research practices for using the Implicit Association Test" (Behavior Research Methods): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170636/

### Mostly unethical

I believe implicit bias testing is mostly unethical in a professional or academic setting, because an organization that puts it there is using it to _assess_ people, and the IAT can't do that. Its test-retest reliability is about .50, so the same person can get a different result next week. Its own creators say it "should not be used for diagnostic purposes." Oswald et al.'s meta-analysis found it was a poor predictor of discrimination and no better than simply asking people. Forscher et al. found that even when scores change, behavior doesn't.

Requiring it is also coercive: Project Implicit says it is research and "it is not ethical to require that people participate." Week 8 tells us to look at **impact** and use "multiple metrics, not just model accuracy." So measure decisions with adverse impact analysis, structured processes and audits, not individuals' reaction times.

#### Sources

- Oswald et al., 2013, IAT criterion meta-analysis (JPSP 105(2), 171–192): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23773046/
- Oswald et al., 2015, "Small effect sizes of unknown societal significance" (JPSP): https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000023
- Forscher et al., 2019, "A meta-analysis of procedures to change implicit measures" (JPSP): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6687518/
- Greenwald & Lai, 2020, "Implicit social cognition" (Annual Review of Psychology), test-retest r = .50, as summarized in Greenwald et al., 2022: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9170636/
- Project Implicit, Ethical Considerations: https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/ethics.html

### Debate portion

**Opening (about 1 minute):** Thesis, the three points in one sentence each, then one example.

**The three places the argument actually turns:**

1. **Education or assessment?** Side A: awareness tool. Side B: the question says "assess," and that's exactly what it can't do.
2. **Voluntary or required?** Side A: offer it. Side B: once required or disclosed, it's coercion and a data risk.
3. **Compared to what?** Side A: compared to doing nothing. Side B: compared to auditing actual decisions.

**Questions to ask the ethical side:**

- "If the same person can score differently next week, what exactly has the organization 'assessed'?"
- "Its creators say not to require it or ask for results. Would you support an employer that does either?"
- "Forscher found changing scores doesn't change behavior. What's the measurable benefit?"

**Questions to ask the unethical side:**

- "If it's voluntary, private and educational, what's the harm?"
- "Week 8 says we're all biased. How do people become aware of biases they can't see?"
- "Isn't a small effect across thousands of hiring decisions still a large harm?"

**If asked, 'Which position do you actually hold?'**

_Use only when asked for your own judgment outside an assigned side._

I lean **mostly ethical as an optional, private learning tool, but unethical as an assessment**. Required testing, collecting results, or using scores in any decision crosses the line. If an organization wants to reduce bias, adverse impact analysis and structured processes do more.

**If you get stuck:** "Is it teaching, or is it judging people?"
