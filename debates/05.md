# Debate 05 — Neural Network Triage in the Emergency Room

**Course:** SEIS 651: AI Ethics · University of St. Thomas

|                |                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Question**   | Is a neural network that ranks arriving ER patients by urgency an ethical way to triage?                                                     |
| **Position 1** | Use of a neural network for triaging and prioritizing incoming emergency patients is mostly ethical, and should be implemented in EDs.       |
| **Position 2** | Use of a neural network for triaging and prioritizing incoming emergency patients is mostly unethical, and should not be implemented in EDs. |

## Quick card

Read this right before you speak.

|                  | Side A: Mostly ethical                                                                              | Side B: Mostly unethical                                                                                      |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Thesis**       | Human triage already gets about 1 in 3 patients wrong; a validated network can catch who it misses. | Triage is a high-stakes ranking of people; a model nobody can explain or audit shouldn't decide who waits.    |
| **Point 1**      | The comparison is today's nurse-run triage, which is inconsistent and biased too.                   | The black box hides _why_ someone was ranked low, so nobody can catch the error in time.                      |
| **Point 2**      | Neural nets beat the standard index at predicting critical outcomes in large studies.               | Neural nets learn shortcuts and proxies from historical data, and those encode existing bias.                 |
| **Point 3**      | It only ranks; a nurse still triages, and it can be audited in ways a tired human can't.            | "A human stays in the loop" fails under ER pressure: automation bias plus blurred liability.                  |
| **Closing line** | "Rejecting it doesn't remove error from triage. It keeps the errors we can't measure."              | "When the ranking is wrong, a patient waits. We should be able to say why, and someone should answer for it." |

## Preparation

Consider how factors like the **black box problem, explainable AI, data privacy, risk, resourcing, informed consent, compliance, liability and responsibility**, etc., influence each position, as well as other factors not discussed in class.

**Framing reminders:**

- The question is about **neural networks in particular**, not AI in general. Lean on what's specific to them: millions of learned weights, no human-readable rules, strong at finding patterns in messy data, prone to learning shortcuts, sensitive to data shift.
- The tool **doesn't treat anyone**. It produces a priority ranking. But in an ER, order _is_ treatment: being ranked low means waiting, and some conditions (sepsis, stroke, MI) get worse by the minute.
- The baseline isn't "no error." Most US EDs use the **Emergency Severity Index (ESI)**, a 5-level algorithm applied by a triage nurse. That is the real comparison.

**Other factors not discussed in class:**

- **Shortcut learning.** A neural net detecting pneumonia on X-rays learned which hospital the image came from (scanner markers) instead of the disease, and generalized poorly to new sites (Zech et al.). In triage, it might learn "arrived by ambulance" or "seen at 3 a.m." rather than how sick someone is.
- **Self-fulfilling feedback.** If the model ranks someone low, they get less testing and attention, so their record looks less severe, and the next version learns that people like them are low priority.
- **Adversarial gaming.** Once patients or staff learn which inputs raise priority, the inputs change.
- **Hidden demographic signals.** Neural nets can predict a patient's self-reported race from chest X-rays even when experts can't see how (Gichoya et al.). Removing race from the inputs doesn't remove it from the model.
- **Vendor models vs. local reality.** The Epic Sepsis Model, deployed at hundreds of hospitals, missed about two-thirds of sepsis cases when independently validated (Wong et al.).

## Positions

### Mostly ethical

I believe a neural network triage tool is mostly ethical, because the alternative isn't perfect human judgment. In a large 2023 study, ESI triage was wrong about a third of the time, and Black patients were more likely to be undertriaged. Neural networks are good at exactly what triage needs: combining vital signs, symptoms, and history into a prediction of who will get worse. In a study of over 135,000 ED visits, a deep neural network predicted critical care outcomes and hospital admission better than ESI-based triage. Fewer high-risk patients sent to the waiting room means fewer people deteriorating in a chair.

I think the black box concern is real but manageable, and it applies to humans too. The ethical version has conditions: the model only ranks, and a triage nurse can always override it; it's validated on this hospital's patients before launch and monitored for drift; it shows the factors behind each score; it's audited for bias by race, sex, age, and language; and the hospital stays responsible for it, as it does for any protocol. Under those conditions, refusing it means choosing the errors we already have because we can't see them.

### Mostly unethical

I believe a neural network triage tool is mostly unethical because triage decides who waits, and a neural network can't tell anyone why. Its prediction comes from millions of learned weights, not rules a nurse can check. When the score is wrong, the nurse has no way to tell in the moment, and under ER pressure people defer to the machine. Explanation tools only approximate what the network did. For a decision this high-stakes, Rudin argues we should use models people can actually interpret, not explain black boxes after the fact.

I also think neural networks learn the past, including its bias. A widely used health algorithm underrated Black patients' needs because it learned from spending, and those who'd had less access to care looked healthier. Neural nets are even better at finding hidden proxies, and they can break silently when the patient population changes. The Epic sepsis model, used at hundreds of hospitals, missed most sepsis cases when someone independently tested it. Patients in an emergency can't consent or opt out, and when something goes wrong, responsibility gets split between the nurse, the hospital, and the vendor. The people most likely to be ranked wrong are the ones least able to push back.

## Debate prep

### The one idea to remember

Both sides agree today's triage makes mistakes. The debate is whether a neural network's mistakes are **more measurable and fixable** than a human's (ethical), or **harder to see and harder to hold anyone accountable for** (unethical).

### Side A: Mostly ethical

**Thesis:** A validated neural network that ranks patients, with a nurse who can override it, is mostly ethical because it catches high-risk patients human triage misses, and its errors can be measured and corrected.

**Point 1: The baseline is flawed human triage, not perfection.**

- Example: Sax et al. reviewed millions of ED visits and found ESI mistriage around 32%, with undertriage (rating someone less urgent than they are) about 3%. Black patients had higher odds of being undertriaged.
- Say: "Three percent sounds small until you count the patients. That's people having a heart attack in the waiting room right now, with no machine involved."
- Idea behind it: judge by consequences, compared to the real alternative.

**Point 2: Neural nets are good at exactly this pattern problem.**

- Example: Raita et al. used data from over 135,000 ED visits; the deep neural network predicted critical care outcomes and hospitalization better than the conventional ESI-based approach, and reduced the number of critically ill patients triaged as low acuity.
- Say: "A nurse sees a few numbers at once. The model sees how this set of vitals, plus this history, turned out across a hundred thousand patients."
- Idea behind it: beneficence. If a tool reliably finds the sickest people sooner, there's a duty to use it.

**Point 3: It's auditable in ways a human isn't.**

- Example: You can test the model on every patient group, log every score, and retrain it. You can't retrain implicit bias out of a tired nurse at 4 a.m. HHS's Section 1557 rule now requires providers to identify and reduce discrimination risks in patient care decision support tools.
- Say: "The Obermeyer bias was found _because_ it was an algorithm someone could audit. Human bias in triage mostly goes unmeasured."
- Idea behind it: justice through accountability. What you can measure, you can fix.

**My conditions (why it's "mostly"):**

1. Ranks only. A triage nurse makes the call and can override.
2. Validated on **this** hospital's population before go-live, then monitored for drift.
3. Shows the top factors behind each score.
4. Regular bias audits by race, sex, age, language; results reported.
5. Disclosed to patients; hospital accepts responsibility as for any protocol.

**If they say... → I say...**

- "It's a black box." → "So is clinical intuition. We trust doctors because of outcomes and accountability, and we can measure the model's outcomes far more precisely."
- "It learns historical bias." → "So did the nurses who generated that history. The difference is we can test the model for bias and correct it."
- "Epic's sepsis model failed." → "Because it was deployed without local validation. That's an argument for my conditions, not against the technology."
- "Nurses will just rubber-stamp it." → "Then design for that: nurse assigns acuity first, then sees the score, and disagreement gets flagged for review."
- "Patients didn't consent." → "Nobody consents to ESI either. Triage protocols are part of treatment. Disclosure is right; individual veto in an emergency isn't workable."

**Closing line:** "Rejecting it doesn't remove error from triage. It keeps the errors we can't measure."

### Side B: Mostly unethical

**Thesis:** Neural network triage is mostly unethical because it ranks human beings by urgency using reasoning nobody can inspect, trained on biased history, in a setting where patients can't consent and responsibility is easy to dodge.

**Point 1: The black box is the problem, and XAI doesn't fix it.**

- Example: Caruana et al. built a pneumonia-risk model and found it had learned that asthma _lowers_ death risk, because asthmatics were rushed to the ICU in the training data. They caught it in a readable model; they chose not to deploy the more accurate neural net because they couldn't check it for errors like that.
- Say: "If a neural net learns 'asthma means low risk,' how would a nurse at 2 a.m. ever know?"
- Idea behind it: high-stakes decisions require reasons someone can examine. Rudin and Ghassemi et al. both argue post-hoc explanations give false reassurance.

**Point 2: Neural nets learn shortcuts and proxies, and they break when things change.**

- Example: Zech et al.'s pneumonia CNN learned hospital-specific markers rather than disease. Gichoya et al. found models detect patient race from X-rays in ways experts can't see. Obermeyer found cost-as-proxy underrated Black patients' needs.
- Say: "You can remove race from the inputs. You can't remove it from what the network learns."
- Idea behind it: justice. Harm lands on groups who already get worse care.

**Point 3: Consent, liability, and "human in the loop" fall apart in the ER.**

- Example: The Epic Sepsis Model was used at hundreds of hospitals and, on independent testing, missed about 67% of sepsis patients while firing alerts on 18% of all hospitalized patients. Automation-bias research shows clinicians over-rely on decision support, especially under time pressure (Goddard et al.).
- Say: "When the nurse follows the score, the nurse says 'the system ranked them low,' the hospital says 'the vendor's tool,' and the vendor says 'it's only decision support.' The patient who waited gets nobody."
- Idea behind it: responsibility. An ethical decision needs someone who can be held to account.

**Weak spot / concession:** Human triage is also biased and inconsistent. Don't claim the status quo is fine. Argue that the fix is better human triage, staffing, or an **interpretable** model (Levin's e-triage used a random forest; simple scoring tools exist), not a neural network specifically.

**If they say... → I say...**

- "Human triage is a black box too." → "A nurse can tell you why, can be questioned, and can be disciplined. A weight matrix can't do any of those."
- "It outperformed ESI in studies." → "In retrospective studies at the sites that trained it. Epic's model looked good too, until someone tested it independently."
- "XAI shows the factors." → "It shows an approximation of the factors. An explanation that's wrong but convincing is worse than none."
- "The nurse can override it." → "In theory. In a packed ER, overriding a computer takes confidence and time nobody has. That's automation bias."
- "We'll audit it for bias." → "Audit against what? The ground truth is past triage decisions, which are already biased."

**Closing line:** "When the ranking is wrong, a patient waits. We should be able to say why, and someone should answer for it."

### Compliance and legal anchors (if asked)

- **EMTALA:** Every person who comes to an ED must get a medical screening exam and stabilizing treatment regardless of ability to pay. A ranking can't be used to skip anyone. ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/regulations-guidance/legislation/emergency-medical-treatment-labor-act))
- **HIPAA:** Using patient data for treatment doesn't require separate authorization; sharing it with a vendor requires a business associate agreement. Training a vendor's general model is a harder question. ([45 CFR 164.506](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-C/part-164/subpart-E/section-164.506))
- **FDA:** Software meant to support time-critical decisions, where a clinician can't independently review the basis in time, generally isn't exempt "clinical decision support." That likely makes an ER triage network a regulated medical device. ([FDA CDS guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software))
- **EU AI Act:** Emergency healthcare patient triage systems are listed as **high-risk** (Annex III), requiring risk management, data governance, transparency, and human oversight. ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj))
- **ACA Section 1557:** Covered providers must make reasonable efforts to identify and mitigate discrimination risks from patient care decision support tools. ([45 CFR 92.210](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-92/subpart-C/section-92.210))

### Debate portion

**Opening (about 1 minute):** Thesis, the three points in one sentence each, then one example.

**The three places the argument actually turns:**

1. **Compared to what?** Side A: compared to ESI, which already mistriages. Side B: compared to better staffing or an interpretable model, not just "ESI or neural net."
2. **Is the black box a dealbreaker?** Side A: judge by measured outcomes. Side B: in a high-stakes, time-critical call, you need reasons someone can check.
3. **Does "human in the loop" really work?** Side A: yes, if designed for it. Side B: not under ER pressure, and it lets everyone shift the blame.

**Questions to ask the ethical side:**

- "When the model ranks a patient low, what exactly does the nurse look at to decide it's wrong?"
- "Who's liable when a patient deteriorates after the model ranked them low: the nurse, the hospital, or the vendor?"
- "Why a neural network specifically, rather than an interpretable model that performs almost as well?"
- "How will you know the model has drifted before patients are harmed?"

**Questions to ask the unethical side:**

- "ESI mistriages about a third of patients today. How many people should wait longer so we can avoid a model?"
- "Is a nurse's intuition any more explainable than a model you can test on every patient group?"
- "If the model were proven better and fairer at your hospital, would you still reject it? Why?"
- "Who should be liable today when a nurse undertriages someone?"

**If asked, 'Which position do you actually hold?'**

_Use only when asked for your own judgment outside an assigned side._

I lean **mostly ethical, but only with conditions**, and the conditions carry most of the weight: rank-only, nurse decides first, local validation, drift monitoring, published bias audits, and clear hospital responsibility. Without those, I'd side with "unethical," because the Epic sepsis case shows what happens when a vendor model ships without local testing. I'd also want the hospital to show the neural network beats an interpretable model by enough to justify losing explainability.

**If you get stuck:** "Compared to what, and who answers when it's wrong?"
